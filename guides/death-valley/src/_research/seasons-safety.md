## NPS seasonal characterizations (verbatim, structured data feed `weatherText`, fetched 2026-09-17)
- "**SPRING** is the most popular time to visit Death Valley. Warm and sunny days with the possibility of
  spring wildflowers is a big attraction."
- "**SUMMER** starts early in Death Valley. By May the valley can be scorching hot."
- "**AUTUMN** arrives in late October, with warm but pleasant temperatures and generally clear skies."
- "**WINTER** has cool days, chilly nights and rarely, rainstorms. With snow capping the high peaks and low
  angled winter light, this season is especially beautiful for exploring the valley."

## THE summer hiking rule
Repeated verbatim on nearly every NPS Death Valley trail/place page (Badwater, Mesquite Flat Sand Dunes,
Golden Canyon, Mosaic Canyon, Natural Bridge, Ubehebe Crater, Harmony Borax Works), all fetched 2026-09-17:
> **"HIKING NOT ADVISED AFTER 10 AM IN THE SUMMER"**
Supporting text from https://www.nps.gov/deva/planyourvisit/safety.htm and
https://www.nps.gov/deva/planyourvisit/summer.htm (fetched 2026-09-17):
- "**DO NOT hike in the valley/lower elevations when it is hot!**" Mountains are the summer refuge —
  NPS names **Wildrose Peak** and **Telescope Peak** as the appropriate summer hikes.
- "Stay on paved roads and close to your vehicle." "Remain near your air-conditioned vehicle whenever possible."
- Park FAQ: "Summer hiking is not recommended except in the early morning hours and in the mountains."
- Badwater thingstodo page: in summer, not recommended to be outside the vehicle longer than **15 minutes**.

## Heat and water
- "**DRINK plenty of water, at least one gallon a day**, to avoid dehydration." The summer guide says
  "at least one gallon (4 liters) of water per day," more if active. Park FAQ says "drink at least 2 to 4
  liters per day, more if you are active in the heat." Also eat salty foods or drink electrolytes to
  replace salt lost in sweat. (Slight internal inconsistency in NPS's own numbers; guide uses "at least
  one gallon / 4 liters per person per day," the figure stated on both the safety and summer pages.)
- Drinking water exists only at **Furnace Creek, Stovepipe Wells and Panamint Springs**.
- **Heat exhaustion**: nausea, vomiting, fatigue, headache, cramps, pale/cool/clammy skin.
  **Heat stroke**: the same plus behavioral change — confusion, disorientation. Response: stop activity,
  get out of the heat, sip water. "Heat illness can be severe and sometimes deadly."
- Temperatures "can reach 130°F (54°C)" (safety page). Devils Golf Course page: mid-May to mid-September
  "temperatures reaching 120 F or higher." Record **134°F (57°C), July 10, 1913, Furnace Creek Ranch** —
  the world record highest air temperature.
- Mesquite Spring Campground (1,800 ft): "nightly lows in the 90s Fahrenheit" in summer.

## Vehicles and breakdowns
Source: https://www.nps.gov/deva/planyourvisit/safety.htm, .../directions.htm, .../backcountryroads.htm — fetched 2026-09-17
- **"The most dangerous thing in Death Valley is not the heat - it is the single car rollover."**
  "Most deaths result from single vehicle rollover accidents on long, winding roads."
  Many roads were built in the 1930s: "narrow and serpentine and cannot be driven at high speed."
- "Follow all posted speed limits, shift to low gear on steep downhill grades, and always wear your seatbelt!"
- If your vehicle breaks down: **stay with it**. Leave the hood up and/or mark the road with a large X
  visible from the air. If you walk out, stay on main roads, carry enough water, and go after sundown.
  Leave a dated note with your plan. "Towing charges are high and AAA often doesn't cover tows on dirt roads."
- Carry extra water for you and the vehicle; tire-changing equipment; two full-size spares in the backcountry.
- Summer backcountry travel (April through mid-October) "can be dangerous and requires additional water
  and supplies and knowledge of how to survive if your vehicle becomes disabled."

## Flash floods
- "**DO NOT HIKE in canyons during an active rainstorm, or if there is a high chance of precipitation.**
  Floods occur quickly with unpredictable large debris; seek higher ground immediately if trapped."
- Mosaic Canyon page: "the site of frequent flash floods (**do not enter if there is a chance of rain!**)."
- Titus Canyon: "Canyon prone to flash flooding, avoid entering when rain threatens."
- Context: annual rainfall is only 1.94 in, but the August 20, 2023 (Hurricane Hilary) and later storms
  destroyed roads across the park — damage on Darwin Falls Road, Lower Wildrose, North Highway, southern
  Badwater Road, Titus Canyon, Emigrant's water system and Scotty's Castle is still unrepaired in Sept 2026.

## Abandoned mines
- "**Do not enter mine tunnels or shafts. Mines may be unstable, have hidden shafts, pockets of bad air,
  and poisonous gas.**"
- Titus Canyon page, on Leadfield: "Many of the mines are open, but enter at your own risk. Loose rocks,
  rotten timbers, unexpected vertical shafts, and animals seeking shelter are potential hazards."
- Mined here historically: gold, silver, lead, zinc, antimony, fluorspar, cinnabar, epsom salts, mercury,
  tungsten, copper, borax, talc, sodium chloride, manganese.

## Wildlife and night driving
- "DO NOT FEED wildlife." Potentially dangerous animals (park FAQ): **rattlesnakes, scorpions, black widow
  spiders, bees, wasps, coyotes, mountain lions**. "Always look first before placing your hands or feet anywhere."
- Commonly seen: coyotes, ravens, roadrunners, ground squirrels, lizards; bighorn sheep in the mountains;
  pupfish at Salt Creek in spring.
- Avoid old buildings with rodent evidence — **hantavirus** risk.
- Sidewinder rattlesnakes live in the dunes; watch your step and do not disturb burrows.
- Roads are unlit, narrow and winding, with no shoulders in places and animals active at dawn/dusk and at
  night. NPS does not publish a specific night-driving rule — the guide's advice to avoid long night drives
  is framed from the rollover statistic and the wildlife/road facts above, not asserted as an NPS rule.

## Communication / self-rescue
- "There is **limited to no cell phone service**." "Help may be many hours away; **travel prepared to
  self-rescue**." Backcountry travelers should carry a satellite messenger or PLB and leave an itinerary
  with someone not on the trip. Dial 911 if you have any signal.

## Winter at elevation
- Dante's View (5,475 ft at the viewpoint per the 5,575 ft-above-Badwater figure), Wildrose (4,100 ft),
  Thorndike (7,400 ft), Mahogany Flat (8,200 ft) and Telescope Peak (11,049 ft) are a different climate:
  temperatures fall **3–5°F per 1,000 ft** of elevation gained (NPS weather page).
- Thorndike: "typically open from mid-April through December, pending snowfall"; "May still be impacted by
  snowy/icy conditions in late spring." Mahogany Flat: "often closed in the winter due to snowy/icy conditions."
- Backcountry-roads page: "In the higher elevations, snow and ice conditions may require tire chains."
- **Telescope Peak in winter is a mountaineering objective**, not a day hike: 12.2 mi, 3,375 ft gain,
  NPS difficulty "Difficult," from a trailhead reached by a dirt road that is often snowed in.
  UNVERIFIED: NPS does not on the pages fetched explicitly require ice axe/crampons for a winter ascent;
  the guide says conditions can require winter mountaineering gear without attributing a rule to NPS.
- Winter is also when the low sun and snow-capped Panamints make the valley most photogenic (NPS weatherText).

## Other rules worth knowing
- **Pets are not allowed on any trail in Death Valley National Park, even if carried**, and may not be left
  in a vehicle. They are allowed on roads and in developed areas on a leash no longer than 6 ft, and — per
  the FAQ — on backcountry dirt roads such as Twenty Mule Team Canyon Road and Titus Canyon Road.
- **No boating or flotation devices anywhere in the park**, including on Lake Manly when it floods Badwater.
- **Driving off designated roads is prohibited** parkwide.
- Do not leave toilet paper in the canyons or on the dunes.
