Mecca Hills — an easy overnight starter trip, October 2026

Hosted by Aaron · Potential trip — planning stage, dates not locked

One night, roughly two and a half hours from Huntington Beach, in a canyon most people from here have never heard of. We camp in the wash in Painted Canyon, walk the ladder slot canyon in the last light of Saturday, cook a real dinner in the sand, and sing until the moon sets. No fees, nothing to book, and you can be home by lunchtime Sunday. We did this in April 2023 with cousins, friends and DLI familia — the photos are in the Photos tab.

This one is a proposal. The dates and plan below are what we have in mind — say if you’re interested and we’ll lock it in.

Home base · campsite

Painted Canyon Road, Mecca Hills

A pull-out in the wash on the upper part of Painted Canyon Road, somewhere in the last mile or two before the parking area at the end — we pick the spot when we get there, because there are no numbered sites and no map of them. Canyon walls on both sides, sand underfoot, about 590 feet above sea level. There is no water, no power, no trash service, no shade and no guaranteed restroom. The trailhead for Saturday's hike is a short drive or a walk up the road from wherever we land, and the nearest supermarket is back in Coachella or Indio, 15 to 25 minutes up the highway.

Getting in

Saturday October 17: leave Huntington Beach around midday. CA-91 east, then I-10 east past Palm Springs to Indio, then south on CA-86 or CA-111 through Coachella to Mecca. Turn east on 66th Avenue, which becomes Box Canyon Road, then left onto Painted Canyon Road and about four miles of dirt. Fill the tank and buy all your food, ice and water in Coachella or Indio — there is nothing after that. Roughly 2½ to 3 hours of driving, which is an estimate, not a promise. Aim to be in camp by 2 p.m. so we can set up and start the ladders loop around 3:30, finishing at sunset.

Getting out

Sunday October 18: a slow morning, a short walk back up the canyon before it heats up, then pack out every scrap and drive home. Two good detours if you want to make a day of it: the Salton Sea shoreline half an hour south, or Box Canyon Road east to Joshua Tree's south entrance and home on I-10 — the long way around, and the better drive. Or make Sunday a full day: The Living Desert in Palm Desert opens at 8 a.m. and is about an hour from camp on the way home.

What these dates mean

Weather

Mid-October at nearby Thermal normally runs about 90°F in the afternoon and 56°F at dawn — a 34-degree swing in one day. October rain normals are under a fifth of an inch, so it is almost certainly dry, but a desert storm is the one thing that would cancel this trip.

Daylight

On Saturday the sun rises at 6:51 a.m. and sets at 6:08 p.m., with usable twilight until about 6:33. The plan is the ladders loop in the last light: start around 3:30 p.m. so the ridge is in golden hour and we are out of the slot and back at camp right around sunset. Everyone carries a headlamp anyway.

The drive in, and what car works

The last four miles are dirt, and BLM officially calls Painted Canyon Road four-wheel-drive access. In dry weather ordinary cars do drive the graded parts, but the real hazard is soft sand: keep moving through it, never stop in it, and walk a pull-out before driving into it. If your car is low or you would rather not risk it, park where the ground is firm and ride the last bit with one of us.

Camp life: water, toilets, trash

Bring everything, starting with a gallon of water per person per day plus cooking and washing water. There is no water and no store. Campers report a primitive pit toilet near the camping area, but no official source lists one — so bring a sealable human-waste bag (a wag bag) or a trowel, bury waste 6 to 8 inches deep and well away from the wash, and pack out your paper. Everything you bring in goes home with you.

No campfire — bring a stove

This is the one that surprises people. BLM's seasonal fire restrictions run April 15 through October 29, 2026, and our dates fall inside them: campfires are limited to approved fire pits at developed recreation sites, which our wash is not. Portable stoves and lanterns with shut-off valves are still fine. There are old fire rings out there; they are not permission. Everyone cooking needs a free California Campfire Permit, which covers stoves too — grab one online before you leave home.

Flash floods and heat

Slot canyons drain the hills above them, and rain you cannot see can fill one. If rain shows up anywhere in the regional forecast, we do not go into the narrows and we do not drive the dirt road — it washes out. Heat is the other one: 90°F with no shade is why the hike is an evening hike, and why BLM tells people to carry at least a liter each plus electrolytes.

Crowds, cell service and closures

Painted Canyon is busy on a good fall Saturday and quiet by dinner time. BLM says the area has little to no cell service — download your maps before you turn off the highway and agree on a meeting time instead of planning to text. Nothing is currently closed here; BLM publishes no road-status page for this unit, so Aaron will call the field office at 760-833-7100 the week before.

Questions people ask before saying yes

Is this good for first-time campers and kids?
Yes — it is close to the ideal first trip. One night, your car is parked next to your tent, the drive home is short if anything goes sideways, and the headline hike is a few miles on flat sand with ladders that kids treat as a playground. The two honest caveats: there is no guaranteed bathroom, and there is no water, so everything comes with you.
How hard are the ladders?
They are wooden ladders leaning in dry waterfalls, the tallest around twelve to fifteen feet, and you climb them with your hands free. Most people find them fun rather than frightening. Nobody maintains them — hikers report the occasional missing rung — so we test each one and spot the kids. Anyone who would rather not can walk up Big Painted Canyon instead, which is just as colorful with no climbing at all.
What car do I need?
BLM calls Painted Canyon Road four-wheel-drive access, and in soft sand that is the right advice. In dry October weather ordinary cars regularly drive the graded stretches — the trick is momentum, not clearance. If your car is low or you would rather not chance it, park on firm ground down the road and ride the last stretch with one of us. Nobody gets left out over a car.
Is there a bathroom?
Assume no. Campers report a primitive pit toilet near the camping area, but no official BLM source lists one, so we plan as if it does not exist: a sealable human-waste bag (a wag bag) or a trowel, waste buried 6 to 8 inches deep and 200 feet from the wash, and all paper packed out. The nearest flush toilets are at the Salton Sea State Recreation Area visitor center, about half an hour away.
What if it is hot, or it rains?
Hot we plan around: 90°F is a normal October afternoon, so we hike from 3 p.m. and let the middle of the day go. Rain is different — rain cancels it. Water funnels into slot canyons from hills you cannot see, and the dirt road washes out. If the regional forecast has anything in it, Aaron will move the weekend rather than risk it.

Why go

Mecca Hills is 26,242 acres of badlands maze wrinkled up by the San Andreas Fault, an hour past Palm Springs and fifteen miles southeast of Indio. BLM describes it plainly: a labyrinth of small, narrow, steep canyons cut into layers of eroded rock, some of it over 600 million years old, with sandy washes full of ironwood, smoke trees and paloverde.

The reason people come is Painted Canyon. A dirt road runs four miles up a wash into striped red, ochre and gray walls and stops at a parking area. From there a slot called Ladder Canyon climbs through cracks barely wider than your shoulders, using wooden ladders bolted into dry waterfalls, then pops out on a ridge with the Salton Sea laid out below and drops back down the wide floor of Big Painted Canyon.

It is free, it is unmanaged, and it is close: there are no designated trails, no water, no ranger, and little to no cell service.

When to go

Best

Spring

Mar–May

Highs 81–95°F · Lows 49–63°F

The classic season and the busiest. March and April are close to perfect for the canyons — warm afternoons, cool mornings, almost no rain. By mid-May the valley is already at 95°F and the canyon floor holds heat long after the sun leaves it. Wildflowers follow a wet winter and are never a given.
Hard

Summer

Jun–Aug

Highs 103–107°F · Lows 69–76°F

Don't. July normals at nearby Thermal are 107°F by day and 76°F at night, there is no shade in the washes, no water anywhere, and no cell service to call for help. Late-summer thunderstorms also put water into slot canyons. If you go at all, it is a sunrise drive-and-look, not a hike.
Best

Fall · our trip

Sep–Nov

Highs 79–102°F · Lows 45–68°F

The season swings hard. September still averages 101°F and behaves like summer; by November the valley is at 79°F with 45°F nights, which is ideal. October is the crossover — 90°F afternoons and 57°F dawns — so hike early or late and let the middle of the day go. Seasonal fire restrictions usually run into late October.
Best

Winter

Dec–Feb

Highs 69–74°F · Lows 38–43°F

Shirtsleeves at noon, near freezing before dawn, and the lowest sun of the year raking across the canyon walls. The one catch is rain: winter is the wettest time here, and this is a place where rain closes the dirt road and puts water through the slots. Watch the forecast and stay out if anything is coming.

1

Ladder Canyon

HikeModerateNo permit
The reason to come. A narrow crack off the main wash climbs through conglomerate walls that close to shoulder width overhead, and where the canyon hits a dry waterfall, a wooden ladder gets you up it — several of them, the tallest around twelve to fifteen feet. It is the rare desert hike that feels like an obstacle course and needs no rope, no permit and no skill beyond a steady foot. Recreation.gov describes Painted Canyon as a moderate hike of up to five miles round trip; secondary trail guides put the full loop at 4.5 to 5 miles.

Painted Canyon and the road in

AreaEasyNo permit

3

Big Painted Canyon

CanyonEasyNo permit
The wide, sand-floored canyon that the loop comes back down, and a fine destination on its own. The walls are the most colorful in the range — bands of rust, cream and slate folded at angles that make the geology obvious to a nine-year-old. Short side canyons branch off it, one reached by its own ladder, and they are usually empty even when the slot is busy. Because it needs no climbing, this is the version of Painted Canyon you can walk with small kids, or in flip-flops you will regret.

Salton Sea State Recreation Area

AreaEasyNo permit
California's largest lake, sitting in a basin about 220 feet below sea level, with the Chocolate Mountains behind it and one of the busiest bird shorelines on the Pacific Flyway. It is also a place in visible decline — the water is shrinking and saltier every year, Varner Harbor is closed to boats, and the beach is made of pulverized barnacle shell. Strange, enormous and worth an hour on the way in or out. Day use is $7 per vehicle, $5 for seniors 62 and over.

Box Canyon Road

Scenic driveEasyNo permit
The paved road that gets you to Painted Canyon is a destination in its own right. It threads a narrow, walled canyon through the southern Mecca Hills — the non-wilderness corridor BLM says splits off the section holding Sheep Hole Oasis and Hidden Springs Canyon — and climbs steadily to Joshua Tree's south entrance at I-10. It was once US 60 and US 70, the main road east out of Los Angeles, before the freeway went elsewhere and the state gave it up.

Coachella Valley Preserve

WalkEasyNo permit
An 880-acre preserve in the Indio Hills where the San Andreas Fault forces groundwater to the surface and a grove of California fan palms grows out of the open desert. The boardwalk and sand paths of the McCallum Trail lead to a palm-shaded pool that looks imported from somewhere much wetter. It protects the Coachella Valley fringe-toed lizard, desert pupfish and Coachella Valley milkvetch. Free to enter, donations welcome — and open on a Sunday morning, which is the point.

Plan your visit

Getting there

FromRoute & timeMiles
Huntington Beach and the LA/OC areaEast on CA-91 through Riverside, then I-10 east (or CA-60 to I-10) over the pass past Palm Springs to Indio. From Indio take CA-86 or CA-111 south through Coachella to Mecca, turn east on 66th Avenue, which becomes Box Canyon Road, and watch for Painted Canyon Road on the left. Roughly 2½ to 3 hours in normal traffic — that figure is approximate, since no official source publishes a drive time; BLM gives the one hard number, 15 miles southeast of Indio.
The back way from I-10BLM's other listed approach: leave I-10 at Cottonwood Springs Road near Chiriaco Summit, by Joshua Tree's south entrance, and take Box Canyon Road southwest through the Mecca Hills to Painted Canyon Road. Paved, two lanes, and a far better drive than the valley route — but it adds time from the coast. Save it for the way home.
The last four milesPainted Canyon Road runs north off Box Canyon Road for about four miles of dirt to the parking area at the end. BLM calls it four-wheel-drive access. The first couple of miles cross tribal land before reaching BLM ground, so camp in the upper part of the road near the trailhead.

Getting around

Everything here is reached by car from camp, and the canyons are walked from the parking area at the end of Painted Canyon Road. Inside the wilderness there are no vehicles and no bicycles — BLM prohibits motor vehicles, off-highway vehicles, bicycles, carts and aircraft landings on all designated wilderness. Through October 29, 2026 the seasonal fire order separately bans motor vehicles off established roads and trails across the whole district. Stay on the roadbed, park on firm ground, and walk from there.

There are no trail signs, no junction markers and no maintained tread. BLM says it directly: no designated trails. Download an offline map before you leave the highway.

Entrance fees & passes

Nothing to pay. BLM lists fees as N/A for Mecca Hills Wilderness — there is no entrance station, no day-use fee and no camping fee, and nothing to reserve.

The neighbors do charge. Joshua Tree National Park is $30 per private vehicle for 7 days ($25 motorcycle, $15 per person on foot or bike, free under 16, $55 for the park annual pass), and entrance stations take card only. Salton Sea State Recreation Area day use is $7 per vehicle, $5 for seniors 62 and over. Coachella Valley Preserve is free.

Reservations & timed entry

None, and none possible. There is no campground, no reservation system and no designated site anywhere on Painted Canyon Road. Camping is first come, first served in the pull-outs along the road, and busy weekends fill the good spots by late afternoon. If you want something you can book, Salton Sea State Recreation Area is about half an hour away.

Fuel, food, water, cell

There is nothing out here. No water, no store, no fuel, no ranger station and no visitor center anywhere in the wilderness or on Painted Canyon Road. BLM lists no facilities for the unit and tells visitors to bring their own water.

Fill the tank and buy every scrap of food, ice and water in Coachella or Indio, 15 to 25 minutes back up the highway, where the full supermarkets are. Mecca itself is a small town with limited services — treat it as a last-chance top-up, not a plan.

The nearest staffed public building to camp is the Salton Sea State Recreation Area visitor center, open 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. daily with restrooms. For road and fire status, call the BLM Palm Springs–South Coast Field Office at 760-833-7100; the public room is open 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. weekdays.

How to book
Nothing to book and nothing to pay. BLM lists fees as N/A for Mecca Hills Wilderness and publishes no campground, no designated sites and no reservation system here — you drive in, find an unoccupied pull-out and stop. BLM's general rule for dispersed camping applies: up to 14 days in any 28-day period, camp within 150 feet of the designated route, use ground that is already disturbed, make no new tracks, and pack everything out.

Two practical catches. First, the first couple of miles of Painted Canyon Road cross tribal land before reaching BLM ground, so carry on up the road before setting up. Second, the good spots go by late afternoon on a nice weekend — arrive with daylight to spare so you can scout a pull-out on foot before driving into it.

Safety & know-before-you-go

The handful of things that actually hurt people here, with the specific rule that prevents each one — sorted for our dates (mid October).

For our dates

critical

Heat, and the one-liter floor

BLM's alert for this exact area: bring at least one liter per person plus electrolytes, start early because temperatures rise quickly by early afternoon, hike with a buddy, and tell someone when you expect back. One liter is the floor for a short walk, not a plan. Mid-October normals at nearby Thermal are 90°F by day; June through August run 103–107°F. There is no shade in the washes and no water anywhere in the wilderness.

critical

Flash floods in the slots

A slot canyon is a drain. Rain falling miles away and out of sight can fill it, and the same water takes out the road behind you — a 2018 storm destroyed Box Canyon Road and it took a $3 million repair to reopen in May 2019. The rule is simple: if rain is anywhere in the forecast for the region, do not enter the narrow canyons and do not drive in. Painted Canyon Road closes after rain.

important

The ladders are not maintained

Nobody official owns the ladders. BLM does not list them, inspect them or vouch for them, and hikers report that a rung or two is sometimes missing and that the tallest is around twelve to fifteen feet. Test each one with your weight before you commit, send the strongest person first and last, and spot children. If a ladder looks wrong, turn around — Big Painted Canyon is a full outing on its own with no ladders in it.

important

Getting lost in the maze

BLM describes Mecca Hills as a badlands labyrinth, a natural maze of small, narrow, steep canyons, and states flatly that there are no designated trails. Side canyons look like the main canyon. Download an offline map before you leave the highway, keep the group together at every junction, and note where you entered the slot from the wash — that junction is easy to miss on the way back.

important

No cell service, no quick help

BLM: this area has little to no cell service. Coverage varies by carrier near the road entrance and disappears in the canyons. Nobody is coming quickly. Tell someone at home your plan and your return time, carry a first-aid kit and more water than you think, and agree on a meeting place and time with your group rather than assuming you can text.

important

Soft sand and the drive in

BLM describes Painted Canyon Road as four-wheel-drive access. In dry conditions ordinary cars regularly make the graded stretches, but the failure mode is sand, not slope: keep momentum, do not stop in a soft patch, and scout every pull-out on foot before driving into it. Carry a shovel and traction boards if you have them. If in doubt, park where the ground is firm and ride the last stretch with someone else.

important

Fires and the campfire permit

The BLM California Desert District is under seasonal fire restrictions from April 15 through October 29, 2026: campfires only in approved fire pits at developed recreation sites, with portable stoves and lanterns with shut-off valves still allowed. Old fire rings in the wash are not permission. A free California Campfire Permit is required year-round for any campfire, barbecue or gas stove on BLM land, and campfires are suspended outright during a Red Flag Warning.

note

Cold, wind and the overnight swing

The valley swings about 34°F between afternoon and dawn in October — 90°F down to 57°F — and December and January dawns run near 38°F. Campers also report that the wind picks up hard in the wash at night, so stake tents properly and weight anything light. It is not dangerous cold, but it is the difference between a good night and a long one.

Frequently asked

About the park in general. Trip questions are on The Trip.

Do I need a permit or a pass?
Not to hike or camp. BLM lists fees as N/A for Mecca Hills Wilderness — no entrance station, no day-use fee, no camping fee. The one piece of paperwork is a free California Campfire Permit, which is required year-round for any campfire, barbecue or gas stove on BLM land outside a developed campground. It takes a few minutes online and covers your calendar year.
Can I drive a normal car to the trailhead?
BLM describes Painted Canyon Road as four-wheel-drive access, and that is the official line. In practice, in dry weather, plenty of ordinary cars drive the graded stretches — the danger is soft sand rather than rocks or steepness. Keep moving through sandy patches, do not stop in them, scout pull-outs on foot, and after rain do not attempt it at all. If you are unsure, park where the ground is firm and ride in with someone else.
Is there a bathroom, or water?
No water — bring all of it. There is no store, no spigot and no ranger station anywhere in the wilderness. On toilets: campers report a primitive pit toilet near the Painted Canyon camping area, but no official BLM source lists one, so plan as if there is none. Bring a sealable human-waste bag (a wag bag) or a trowel, bury waste 6 to 8 inches deep and 200 feet from any wash or trail, and pack out your paper.
How hard is Ladder Canyon, really?
Moderate. It is roughly 4.5 to 5 miles with about 600 feet of climbing, on sand, with several ladders — the tallest around twelve to fifteen feet — and a few scrambles. Fit kids who like climbing love it; anyone very afraid of heights or tight spaces will not. The no-ladders version is walking up Big Painted Canyon and back, which is genuinely easy and just as colorful.
When should I go?
October through April. Nearby Thermal averages 90°F in October, drops to 79°F in November and 69–74°F in midwinter, then climbs back through the 80s and 90s in spring. June through August averages 103 to 107°F with no shade and no water — that is not a hiking season, it is a hazard. Winter brings the most rain, which is the other thing to watch here.
Can I bring my dog?
On BLM land, yes — but think about the route. Getting a dog up and down ladders and dry falls is hard and can be dangerous, the rock is sharp on paws, and there is no water. Big Painted Canyon is the reasonable dog walk. Note that Coachella Valley Preserve bans pets entirely apart from service dogs, so do not plan both in one day.
What is Chuckwalla National Monument, and does it change anything?
In January 2025 the Mecca Hills Wilderness was included in the new Chuckwalla National Monument, about 624,270 acres of BLM land holding five wilderness areas. For a visitor nothing has changed: same field office, same rules, still no fee. The monument is being challenged in court — in March 2026 conservation groups and Tribal Nations were allowed to join the case to defend it — but access today is unaffected.
